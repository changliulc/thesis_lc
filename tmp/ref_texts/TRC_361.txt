7 Abstract
8 Sensys Networks’ VDS240 vehicle detection system is a wireless sensor network composed of a collection of 3 00 by 300 by
9 200 sensor nodes put in the center of a lane and a 6 00 by 4 00 by 4 00 access point (AP) box placed 15 0 high on the side of the
10 road. A node measures changes in the earth’s magnetic ﬁeld induced by a vehicle, processes the measurements to detect the
11 vehicle, and transfers the processed data via radio to the AP. The AP combines data from the nodes into information for
12 the local controller or the Traﬃc Management Center (TMC). An AP communicates via radio directly with up to 96 nodes
13 within a range of 150 0; a Repeater extends the range to 1000 0. This range makes it suitable to deploy VDS240 networks for
14 traﬃc counts, stop-bar and advance detection, and measurement of queue lengths on ramps and at intersections, as well as
15 parking guidance and enforcement. VDS240 is self-calibrating, IP-addressable and remotely monitored. Data are not lost
16 because unacknowledged data packets are retransmitted. The accuracy of VDS240 for vehicle counts, speed and occu-
17 pancy is comparable to that of well-tuned loops. Because the nodes report individual vehicle events, the AP also calculates
18 individual vehicle lengths, speeds and inter-vehicle headways—measurements that can be used for new traﬃc applications.
19 In July 2007, VDS240 systems were deployed in arterials and freeways in several cities and states, and 30 customer trials
20 were underway in the US, Australia, Europe and South Africa.

92 The AP is the ‘base station’ of the local area wireless network with up to 96 nodes. Its algorithms extract
93 useful information from the data it receives from these nodes. For example, in freeway traﬃc applications, the
94 30-s count is just the number of vehicle arrivals at a node during 30 s; the 30-s occupancy is the fraction of

98 The measurement of individual vehicle arrival, departure and speed permit new applications. As one exam-
99 ple, Coifman and Cassidy (2002) use sequences of individual vehicle lengths measured at two locations to esti-
100 mate the travel time between those locations. As another application, Cheung et al. (2005) report an

105 The AP is enclosed in a 6 00 by 600 by 400 box, and mounted between 12 0 and 20 0 high on a pole on the side of
106 the road. It communicates via radio with nodes on the ground at a range of between 90 0 and 175 0. Using a
107 RP240 repeater extends the communication range to 1000 0. The radio transceivers in VSN240, RP240 and
108 AP240 conform to the IEEE 802.15.4 PHY standard. The radio operates in one of sixteen 5 MHz channels
109 in the 2.4–2.48 GHz band. Sensors and repeaters connected to the same AP operate on the same channel. Sen-
110 sors connected to a repeater operate on a diﬀerent channel. A repeater listens to the AP on the ﬁrst channel
111 and to its sensors on the second channel, and relays packets between the two channels.
112 2.3. Communication protocol
113 Communication in the local area network is governed by the Sensys Nanopower Protocol (SNP). SNP is a
114 TDMA (time-division multiple-access) protocol, summarized in Fig. 3. Time is divided into 125 ms-long sub-
115 frames, each subdivided into 64 time slots. Each sensor node is assigned a slot in which it can transmit 50 bytes
116 of data. The ﬁgure shows slot 1 assigned to VSN A and slot 3 to VSN B. (A node that needs to transmit more
117 data, for example in raw mode, is assigned more than one slot.)
118 Four slots are special. Slot 0 is used by the AP to synchronize the node clocks and to send global param-
119 eters for detection, transmit interval, etc. Slot 2 is used for software download. Slots 4 and 34 are used for
120 acknowledgement of sensor data packets by the AP. If a node’s data packet is not acknowledged, it retrans-
121 mits the packet (up to a limit), which protects against data loss even when some transmitted packets are lost.
122 SNP’s tight time synchronization permits the radio in the node to be in ‘sleep’ mode during slots when it is
123 not transmitting or receiving. As a result the radio is awake less than 1% of the time, which enables the long
124 battery lifetime. A detailed study ( Coleri and Varaiya, 2006 ) explains why TDMA consumes a tiny fraction of
125 the energy consumed by the more common CSMA (carrier sense multiple-access) protocol in multi-hop wire-
126 less sensor networks.

154 3. Performance
155 The most fundamental decision made by a traﬃc surveillance system is vehicle detection: the decision
156 whether a vehicle is present or not and, if it is present, the determination of the time instants of the vehicle’s
157 arrival and departure. The quality of all other measurements, such as counts, occupancy and speed, depends
158 on the accuracy of vehicle detection.
159 3.1. Vehicle detection
160 Fig. 6 shows the vertical or z axis of the earth’s magnetic ﬁeld measured by a VSN240 sensor as a truck
161 moves over the node. (This is the vehicle’s z axis magnetic signature.) Because the vehicle causes a rapid
162 change in the magnetic ﬁeld, the vehicle can be detected by a threshold rule: a vehicle arrives when the mag-
163 netic ﬁeld crosses a threshold, say a, from below and it departs when the ﬁeld crosses a slightly lower thresh-
164 old, say b, from above. (The earth’s z axis magnetic ﬁeld strength varies between 0.3 and 0.6 gauss over North
165 America; the default threshold values recommended for VSN240 in North America are a = 40 milligauss,
166 b = 23 milligauss. These values indicate the large ﬂuctuation in the magnetic ﬁeld caused by a vehicle.)
167 Two modiﬁcations are needed to make the threshold rule work in practice.
168 First, the ambient magnetic ﬁeld drifts over time and space. Hence in order to keep ﬁxed thresholds, the
169 ambient ﬁeld must be tracked and the measurements must be centered at the tracked value, as is the case with
170 the plot in Fig. 6 . Tracking is achieved by a ﬁrst-order ﬁlter using sample values only when a vehicle is not
171 present and when the magnetic ﬁeld is not ﬂuctuating signiﬁcantly. Tracking the ambient ﬁeld automates cal-
172 ibration and permits use of a ﬁxed threshold. One consequence is that diﬀerent copies of the VSN240 give the
173 same measurements and inter-sensor node diﬀerences are virtually non-existent. By contrast, inductive loop
174 measurements depend on how the loops were installed and how they are aﬀected by pavement stress over time.
175 Moreover, calibration of loop-based systems in the ﬁeld is almost never done because it is prohibitively expen-
176 sive, requiring expensive video processing to obtain ground truth. As a result, loop-based occupancy and
177 speed measurements suﬀer from an unknown bias, making direct inter-loop comparison of these quantities
178 meaningless ( Jia et al., 2001 ).

179 Second, as seen in Fig. 6, the magnetic ﬁeld may cross the threshold several times during the passage of the
180 same vehicle, so a naı¨ve implementation of the threshold rule will declare multiple vehicles in place of one vehi-
181 cle. The ﬁgure also suggests the remedy: if a threshold up-crossing occurs very quickly after a down-crossing, it
182 is likely that they are associated with the same vehicle. That is, if a down-crossing is followed by an up-cross-
183 ing within a short time interval, say d, the two ‘spurious’ crossings should be ignored. The correct choice of d is
184 a little tricky. Observe that the interval between the spurious crossings depends on the vehicle speed: if the
185 vehicle is going at half the speed, the interval will be twice as long. Thus d must be adapted (inversely) to
186 the speed. Furthermore, as the magnetic signature depends on the spatial distribution of ferrous material in
187 the vehicle, the number of spurious crossings and their location in the signature will vary. So the proper choice
188 of d (as well as a, b) is ﬁnally based on tests with a vehicle signature database. Lastly, the longitudinal or x axis
189 magnetic ﬁeld is also used in detection to reduce the spurious crossings.
190 A vehicle detection system makes two kinds of errors: it overcounts vehicles by mistaking more than one
191 vehicle for a single vehicle; and it undercounts vehicles either by not detecting a vehicle or by ‘merging’ two
192 vehicles into one. Experimental results assessing VD240 performance are given in Section 3.3.

207 The objective of the CCIT study was to evaluate VDS240 as a possible replacement for inductive loops for
208 the measurement of freeway traﬃc parameters. The study focused on data quality measures of timeliness,
209 completeness, validity, and accuracy as deﬁned in the ( FHWA, 2004) report. The FHWA report recommends
210 two additional measures, coverage and accessibility or usability, which are not meaningful in the context of the
211 CCIT study.
212 Data from two inductive loop pairs in lanes 1 and 2 (the two lanes closest to the median) on I-80, near
213 Berkeley, are compared with data from four VSN240 nodes placed in the center of the loops. The loops
214 are maintained by the Berkeley Highway Lab (BHL), which is operated by CCIT. Loop outputs are sampled
215 at 60 Hz. In addition to the loops, video footage served as a ground truth to evaluate count accuracy of the
216 VDS240. Data were collected from 5 AM to 10 PM daily for two full weeks, during June and July, 2006.
217 3.3.1. Timeliness
218 This refers to the time lag between sensed events and the availability of the event data. Two time lags are
219 relevant. The ﬁrst is the delay between a VSN240 radio transmission of the data packet containing vehicle
220 arrival and departure timestamps and the reception of the packet by the AP240 radio. The second is the delay
221 between the access point’s transmission of a data packet via the GPRS cellular system and its reception at the
222 TMC which, in the study, is represented by the SNAPS server (see Fig. 5). This second time lag is independent
223 of the Sensys VDS240. Of course, if the access point is connected via a serial port to a local controller, the
224 second time lag would be absent.
225 Table 1 displays statistics of the time lag between the sensor node and access point: the mean packet deliv-
226 ery time is 0.13 s and 99% of packets are delivered within 0.40 s; the corresponding numbers for the GPRS
227 delay between the access point and the SNAPS server are 1.00 s and 7.06 s.
228 3.3.2. Completeness
229 This refers to the availability to a TMC application of the event data generated at the source, and is mea-
230 sured by the ratio of the number of records received by the application to the expected number of records gen-
231 erated by the source (sensor node). Data incompleteness is caused by sensor failure, failure of the link between
232 sensor node and access point or a GPRS outage between the latter and the SNAPS server. If there is such a
233 failure no detection event will be reported. However, there may also be no detection event. In the absence of
234 ground truth concerning the number of source events, the completeness ratio cannot be calculated, and an
235 alternative test was constructed. It was determined that between 5AM and 10 PM on weekdays it was extre-
236 mely likely that at least one vehicle would pass over each sensor within a 90-s ‘activity interval’. Thus the test
237 compared the fraction of 90-s activity intervals during which the VSN240 nodes and the associated loop detec-
238 tors report events.
239 According to Table 5.2 in ( Margulici et al., 2006 ), the nodes and loop detectors reported events in more
240 than 99% of the 450,000+ activity intervals. This means that there was no 90-s interval during the two week
241 experiment during which either the VSN240 node failed or data were lost by the node-to-access point link fail-
242 ure or by the GPRS link failure. Since we know that both radio links do fail, the absence of data loss implies
243 that both the acknowledgement scheme in the SNP protocol over the VSN240-AP240 link and the TCP imple-
244 mentation over the GPRS link work well.

257 3.3.3.2. Valid occupancy. The valid range of 30-s average occupancy was taken as 0–47%, as recommended by
258 FHWA, and conﬁrmed by loop detector occupancy measurements, 99% of which were within this range. The
259 study found that more than 99% of 25,000+ estimates made by each of the four VSN240 sensors were within
260 this range ( Margulici et al., 2006, Appendix B ).
261 3.3.3.3. Valid volume. The study found that more than 99% of 25,000+ estimates of 30-s volume averages made
262 by each of the four VSN240 sensors were within the range 0–2600 vehicles per hour per lane (Appendix B,
263 Margulici et al., 2006 ).
264 3.3.3.4. Valid volume-occupancy. To determine the validity of the pair (volume, occupancy) 30-s estimates, the
265 study constructs a region in volume-occupancy space that includes 99% of loop detector-based estimates, and
266 found that between 95% and 97% of each of the four VSN240 estimates and 98% of the four loop-based esti-
267 mates belonged to the region (Appendix B, Margulici et al., 2006 ).

269 of the time—almost the same proportion as for the loops.

314 Vehicle detection systems fail and it is important to quickly and remotely detect the failure. Some loop fail-
315 ures, such as chattering, are quickly detected by statistical tests. But crosstalk—in which the detector in one
316 lane reports the presence of vehicles in an adjacent lane—cannot be detected because the reported faulty mea-
317 surements look normal. Similarly, misalignment, which causes radar detectors to incorrectly report measure-
318 ments in an adjacent lane, is diﬃcult to detect. Crosstalk and misalignment can occur because of the poor
319 quality of installation or because of changes over time. VDS240 provides both qualitative and quantitative
320 diagnostic information in the form of RSSI and LQI, both of which can readily be understood and adjusted
321 very simply. As a result VDS240 never confuses traﬃc from one channel to another. At the same time, a sensor
322 failure is quickly detected.
323 In summary, loop-based systems are failure-prone and inaccurate; moreover, their cost of installation and
324 maintenance will grow over time because they require large labor time. Making new investments in obsolete
325 loop technology is ill-advised. Radar-based systems may not provide the accuracy needed for applications like
326 ramp metering; and they cannot be used for traﬃc signal control or stop-bar presence detection or queue mea-
327 surements. They, too, require skilled labor for calibration and maintenance. The VDS240 requires no calibra-
328 tion, uses little labor time during installation, and is remotely maintained. Moreover, it serves all traﬃc
329 surveillance applications depicted in Fig. 1. The lifecycle cost of a VDS240 system is a fraction of a loop-based
330 system. Furthermore, most of the cost of VDS240 is due to electronics manufacture and assembly, both of
331 which will decline with volume. This makes VDS240 the preferred choice for pervasive traﬃc surveillance.
332 4. Conclusion
333 It is a commonplace among professionals that eﬀective implementation of standard ITS schemes of ramp
334 metering and signal control can dramatically reduce congestion. For example, it is estimated that ramp meter-
335 ing could have eliminated 37% of the PM peak congestion in California freeways during October–December,
336 2005 ( PeMS, 2006 ). Congestion reduction also provides large beneﬁts from improved travel time reliability
337 (Cambridge Systematics, 2001 ). Caltrans has recently begun to measure the impact of congestion by lost pro-
338 ductivity—deﬁned as the number of lane–mile–hours of capacity lost due to congestion. The lost productivity
339 of California freeways during October 2006 is estimated at 14,000 lane–mile–hours per day ( PeMS, 2006 ).
340 Because of lack of data, we have no way of estimating congestion loss on streets.
341 Despite the consensus about the eﬃcacy of ITS schemes, they are rarely implemented, even in a progressive
342 state like California. More sophisticated schemes for co-ordinated ramp and signal control, rapid transit, trav-
343 eler information, incident detection and management, parking guidance and demand management, remain
344 conﬁned to research journals with no plans for real-world testing. One reason for this poor implementation
345 record is the lack of adequate detection, in terms of coverage and accuracy. The quality of loop detector data
346 is widely perceived as being so poor that engineers often do not trust the data.
347 Thus implementation of ITS schemes requires a pervasive system of accurate traﬃc sensors. With its high
348 cost, inadequate accuracy and diﬃculty of deployment, loop detector technology—the loops, detector cards,
349 wiring for power and communication, and controllers—is incapable of serving this function. Wireless sensor
350 network technology appears ideally suited. It requires no wires, so that it can be deployed wherever necessary.
351 It is as accurate as well-calibrated loops. It provides individual vehicle measurements, permitting the develop-
352 ment of new applications.
353 VDS240’s low cost, ease of installation and maintenance, ﬂexibility of deployment, accuracy, IP networking
354 and data integrity make it suitable for pervasive traﬃc surveillance. VDS240 is backward ‘loop compatible’: a
355 defective loop, when replaced by a node, appears to the controller like a functioning loop. VDS240 is ‘future
356 proof’. First, as new applications are developed, the software can be downloaded to existing systems. In the
357 future, road system surveillance will include other sensing modalities, such as temperature, pressure, humidity,
358 light, and pollutants, for which low-power, miniature sensors are becoming available. These sensors can be
359 deployed in nodes alongside the magnetic sensor nodes described here, while communicating with the same
