P001: [heading 1] Integrated Sensing–Communication–Computing–Control Research Plan
P002: [heading 2] 1. Literature Survey on Integrated SC³
P003: [First Paragraph] Recent 6G research highlights that synergistic integration of sensing, communication, computing and control (often termed SC³ or ISC³) is crucial for safe, autonomous systems[1][2]. For example, UAV swarm networks are envisioned as “reflex-arc” loops that gather sensor data, process it on-board (at an edge hub), and issue control commands in one closed loop[3]. Other works co-design sensing and control: one study formulates a joint LQR vs. localization optimization for multi‑UAVs, trading off control performance with target sensing (Fisher information)[4]. In wireless control systems, researchers have even applied semantic communications, extracting temporal features of control signals to reduce communication load while maintaining accuracy[5].
P004: [Compact] Integrated SC³ frameworks: Fang et al. model an SC³ closed loop where a UAV’s edge hub handles sensing → computing → control, and jointly optimizes communication and control to minimize a combined LQR cost[3].
P005: [Compact] Co-design of SC and control: Jin et al. formulate a multi‑UAV co-design problem, optimizing resource allocation and deployment so as to minimize a weighted sum of control cost (LQR) and sensing accuracy (Fisher information)[4].
P006: [Compact] Time-sequence semantic control: Wang et al. introduce a time-sequence SC paradigm using an ISC³ architecture, where AI modules at the transmitter and receiver extract semantic features over time and adapt control gains. This reduces unnecessary transmissions and improves control accuracy under limited comms[5].
P007: [First Paragraph] Together, these studies suggest that traditional sensor-fusion or communication-centric designs are insufficient: a joint SC³ approach can significantly improve reliability and efficiency[6][3].
P008: [heading 2] 2. Mathematical Modeling of Integrated Systems
P009: [First Paragraph] We will develop formal models that capture the closed-loop dynamics of sensing, communication, computing and control. One can model the vehicle (or robot) with state-space equations, sensor observations, network channels (with delays/drops), and controller actions in one framework. For instance, Fang et al. treat the system as a networked control loop with an edge “reflex” hub, leading to SC³ loops and an overall LQR cost that incorporates sensing and communication costs[3]. Likewise, we can formulate a joint cost function combining control objectives (e.g. LQR or MPC cost) and sensing/comm metrics (e.g. information gain or detection error). An example is a weighted optimization of LQR cost and the determinant of a Fisher information matrix[4].
P010: [Compact] SC³ reflex-arc model: Represent the vehicle and sensor network as an integrated loop. Define state update equations and an observation model; include communication rates and computing delays. For linear Gaussian cases, this yields a “reflex-arc” LQR formulation[3].
P011: [Compact] Multi-objective cost: Encode control performance (e.g. quadratic state/control cost) and sensing goals (e.g. target localization error) in one objective. Use techniques like alternating optimization or convex-concave programming to solve the non-convex joint allocation problem[4].
P012: [Compact] Markov decision process: Formulate the problem as a (partially observed) MDP where the belief state includes uncertain sensor and comm outcomes. The optimal control problem then balances control costs with information acquisition costs.
P013: [First Paragraph] This modelling will yield key relationships between sensor/communication reliability and control stability. We will derive conditions (e.g. minimum data rates or sensing accuracy) for closed-loop stability or performance guarantees, generalizing networked control theory to the integrated SC³ setting.
P014: [heading 2] 3. Decision-Theoretic Control under Uncertainty
P015: [First Paragraph] Next we will design control policies that explicitly account for sensing and communication uncertainty. Rather than fusing noisy sensor readings into a single decision, a decision-theoretic approach maintains uncertainty and plans accordingly. For instance, one can use a belief state over the environment (e.g. obstacle present or not) and apply a partially observable MDP or Bayesian decision framework. The control action (e.g. slow down, maintain speed) is chosen to maximize expected safety or utility given that belief. This is akin to a rational driver model: if a road sign indicates a possible obstacle (low confidence), the policy may reduce speed to collect more data before deciding.
P016: [Compact] POMDP/Bayesian planning: Model sensor readings and comm messages as noisy observations of the true state. Update beliefs via Bayes’ rule, and compute an optimal policy (e.g. by dynamic programming) that trades off immediate performance vs. information gain (slowing down to confirm a sensor reading).
P017: [Compact] Risk-sensitive criteria: Incorporate safety margins by penalising worst-case outcomes (e.g. collision). Design robust controllers that ensure stability even under packet loss or sensor faults.
P018: [Compact] Learning-based strategies: Leverage reinforcement learning or adaptive algorithms to learn decision rules. For example, recent work integrates information-theoretic measures (mutual information) and memory (LSTM) into a deep multi-agent RL framework, learning when to transmit control updates vs. predict the state[7]. The reward function is crafted to balance communication cost and control accuracy. Such approaches can discover nuanced policies that a human-like cautious driver might follow.
P019: [First Paragraph] Overall, the goal is to optimize end-to-end safety and performance rather than isolated sensor accuracy. Policies will be evaluated on metrics like expected collision probability and average speed under uncertainty.
P020: [heading 2] 4. Simulation and Autonomous Driving Scenarios
P021: [First Paragraph] We will validate our models and policies in realistic simulations of autonomous driving. This entails building a co-simulation environment that includes vehicle dynamics, multi-modal sensors, and wireless communication. For example, one can use CARLA or LGSVL for high-fidelity driving and perception, coupled with SUMO for traffic simulation and MATLAB/ROS for control logic[8][9]. The simulation must support cameras, LiDAR, radar, V2X messages and the integration of control algorithms.
P022: [Compact] Co-simulation platform: Integrate an AD simulator (e.g. CARLA) with traffic (SUMO) and vehicle dynamics (MATLAB/Simulink or CarSim). Ensure sensors (RGB/Degree cameras, LiDAR, radar, GPS) and V2X (vehicle-to-infrastructure) messages can be realistically generated[8][9]. This provides ground-truth data and allows testing of fusion/decision modules.
P023: [Compact] Scenario testing: Implement driving scenarios that stress SC³ decision-making. For example, a sign may intermittently report an obstacle; onboard sensors disagree. Test controllers that either fully fuse the info (may lead to overly conservative or unsafe actions) versus our integrated policy (initial slowdown, followed by confirmation) as in the motivating example. Other scenarios include sensor dropouts or communication delays during obstacle encounters.
P024: [Compact] Performance evaluation: Measure safety (collision rates, safe braking distance), efficiency (travel time, speed), and reliability (correct obstacle detection rate). Compare the integrated SC³ policy against baselines (pure sensor fusion or pure communication strategies).
P025: [First Paragraph] Such simulations will demonstrate how the integrated approach can avoid collisions or minimize performance loss in cases where pure sensing or pure communication would fail. We will also explore real-time constraints to ensure that our control policies can run on automotive or UAV hardware.
P026: [heading 2] 5. Theoretical Framework and Testbeds
P027: [First Paragraph] Finally, we will synthesise a formal theory of integrated SC³ control and propose experimental testbeds. The theoretical framework will unify ideas from networked control, information theory and decision theory. We aim to derive performance bounds (e.g. minimum information rate for stability, or risk bounds under uncertainty) and design principles (e.g. when to trust a remote sensor reading versus local sensors). All claims will be rigorously proved or validated by analysis. This framework will be submitted to leading journals and conferences to establish the foundation of the SC³ field[10].
P028: [Compact] Analytical results: Prove conditions for closed-loop stability and performance in terms of sensing/communication parameters. For instance, derive how communication latency and sensor error jointly affect the LQR cost. Develop control-theoretic guarantees (e.g. ISS or Lyapunov stability) that incorporate communication constraints.
P029: [Compact] Standards and regulation: Engage with standard bodies and industry (as highlighted in recent workshops[10]) to align the research with practical needs. Propose guidelines for deploying ISC³ in automotive or UAV systems (e.g. safety certification criteria under uncertain comm/sensing).
P030: [Compact] Experimental testbeds: Design or leverage physical platforms for real-world tests. Possible testbeds include instrumented vehicles with V2X roadside units and simulated faulty sensors, or UAV fleets with coordinated sensing and control. These setups will allow on-site experiments to validate our policies under real conditions (e.g. outdoors with varying connectivity).
P031: [First Paragraph] Together, this plan will generalise the motivating example into a comprehensive SC³ theory and practice. By iterating between modelling, algorithm design, and experiment, we will establish principled methods for reliable autonomous control despite imperfect sensing and communication[10][3].
